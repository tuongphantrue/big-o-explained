Explained with everyday examples

How your code slows down as the input grows.

Imagine you're looking for a name in a phone book. Depending on how you search, finding one name in a book of 20 pages versus a book of 2,000 pages can feel exactly the same — or a thousand times harder. Big O notation is just a way to describe that difference.

The six you'll actually meet

Six ways of "looking for something," explained simply

Same six complexity classes as the chart above, each with a story a ten-year-old could follow. Cards run from calmest (top border, lightest) to most alarming (darkest).

O(1)

Constant time — "instant"

The storySomeone sticks a note on the phone book cover: "Mom is on page 5." You flip straight there. It doesn't matter if the book has 10 pages or 10,000 — you still get there in one move.

The size of the pile never matters. One step, always.

Real examples: grabbing item #5 from a list, checking a locker by its number, looking up a word you already know the page number for.

O(log n)

Logarithmic time — "guess and narrow down"

The storyYou're hunting for "Tom" in a phone book sorted A-to-Z. You open to the middle. Too early — throw away that whole half. Open the middle of what's left. Too late — throw away that half too. Keep halving until you land on "Tom."

Each guess throws away half the remaining pile. A million names? About 20 guesses.

Real examples: guessing a number between 1-100 by always asking "higher or lower," binary search, finding a word in a real dictionary.

O(n)

Linear time — "check every page"

The storyNow the phone book is NOT sorted — names are scribbled in random order. To find "Tom," you must check every single page until you spot it. No shortcuts.

Twice the pile means twice the work. The effort grows exactly as fast as the pile does.

Real examples: reading every book on a shelf to find one title, counting every kid in a lunch line, scanning a messy list for a name.

O(n log n)

Linearithmic time — "tidy up, then search"

The storyBefore looking anything up, you spend time putting the messy phone book in A-to-Z order first. That tidying costs a bit more than one plain pass through the pile — but once it's sorted, every future search becomes the fast "guess and narrow" kind.

A little more work than O(n) up front, in exchange for much faster searches later.

Real examples: alphabetizing a bookshelf, sorting a deck of cards before dealing, most "sort the list" tools on a computer.

O(n²)

Quadratic time — "everyone shakes everyone's hand"

The storyPicture a classroom where every kid has to shake hands with every OTHER kid, exactly once. 10 kids means about 45 handshakes. 20 kids means about 190. Double the kids, and the handshakes roughly quadruple.

A loop inside a loop. Fine for a small classroom, chaos for a whole stadium.

Real examples: comparing every kid's height to every other kid's, checking every pair of puzzle pieces to see which two fit together.

O(2ⁿ)

Exponential time — "the doubling coins"

The storySomeone offers you a deal: "1 coin today. 2 tomorrow. Then 4, then 8, then 16" — doubling every day. It feels tiny for the first week. By day 20, a single day's coins would number over a million.

Starts sleepy, then explodes almost overnight. Adding just one more item can nearly double the work.

Real examples: trying every possible combination on a padlock, listing every possible team you could pick from a growing group of friends.
